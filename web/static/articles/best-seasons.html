The best IPL seasons ever, on one scale

By Fine Leg XI · 23 November 2026

Arguments about the greatest IPL season usually end where they start. One person points to Virat Kohli's 973 runs in 2016, another to Chris Gayle's hitting in 2011, a third to Lasith Malinga's yorkers, and there is no common unit to settle it: runs, wickets, strike rate and economy do not add up, and a run in 2008 was harder to come by than a run in 2026.

Fine Leg XI gives every player-season one number from 70 to 99, worked out the same way for batters and bowlers and for every year from 2008 to 2026. There are 3,337 of those cards in the game. This article looks at the very top of that list: the 32 seasons rated 99, the highest the scale goes.

How one number covers batting and bowling

The rating starts from the ball, not from the scorecard. For every over of an innings and every number of wickets down, the archive says what an average ball is worth: how often it goes for a dot, a single, a four or a six, and how often it takes a wicket. Each ball a player faced or bowled is compared with that par. A batter who scores faster than par in the situations he actually batted in gains; a wicket costs what a wicket is worth at that point of the innings. A bowler's figures are the same comparison from the other end.

That puts batting and bowling in one unit, runs above an average player, and the season total is turned into runs a match. Each season is then measured against its own league, so a player is judged on how far he stood above the cricketers he actually played against. An ordinary player reads about 78 in any year. Only 7% of all cards are rated 90 or more. The full method is in how the ratings work.

A few things stand out. They come from 16 different seasons, so no single era owns the list. Chris Gayle appears three years running, 2011 to 2013, which nobody else manages. AB de Villiers, David Warner and Sunil Narine each appear three times as well, in years further apart: Narine twelve years apart, as a mystery spinner in 2012 and 2013 and as an opening all-rounder in 2024. The newest names are from 2026, the most recent season in the archive.

Why so many bowlers

9 of the 32 are bowlers and 4 are all-rounders. That surprises people who think of the IPL as a batter's competition, but it follows from measuring everyone against par. A bowler gets at most four overs a match, so his season is fewer balls than a top-order batter's. What he does with each of them can be worth far more than a batter's runs are. Malinga's 2011 (28 wickets), Jasprit Bumrah's 2020 (27 wickets) and Bhuvneshwar Kumar's 2026 (28 wickets) reach the same top mark as the best batting seasons: measured ball by ball against par, they were worth as much to their sides.

The bowlers on the list share a pattern: wickets AND economy. A bowler who takes wickets but is expensive gives back much of what the wickets were worth, and one who is cheap but rarely strikes leaves the batters at the crease. The seasons at 99 did both.

Not every famous season makes it

Kohli's 2016 is at the top, 973 runs off 640 balls, still the most runs anyone has made in an IPL season. So are Shubman Gill's 2023 (890 runs off 564) and Jos Buttler's 2022 (863 off 579). Vaibhav Sooryavanshi's 2026 is there too, with 776 runs off just 327 balls.

A season of big totals is not enough on its own, though. Runs made quickly, in situations where they were hard to make, count for more than the same runs made slowly or in a dead game. And the same player can be at both ends of the scale: Kohli's 2022, 341 runs off 294 balls, is rated 72. The rating is about one season, never the name on the card.

A ceiling, not a tie

Why do 32 seasons share the top number instead of one standing alone? Because 99 is a ceiling. The scale is stretched so the strongest seasons reach the top and an ordinary one sits in the high 70s, and anything beyond the top mark is held at 99 rather than pushed past it. The cards at 99 are the seasons that cleared that mark. They are not exactly equal, and the match engine plays each one with its own measured numbers, so Gayle's 2012 and Malinga's 2011 still behave very differently in a match.
