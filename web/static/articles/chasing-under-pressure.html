Chasing under pressure

By Fine Leg XI · 23 November 2026

A chase is the one time in a T20 match when a batter knows exactly what he needs. The scoreboard tells him how many runs and how many balls are left, and every ball he faces is a small decision about how much risk that number calls for. Fall behind and he has to attack. Stay ahead and he can afford to wait for the bad ball.

We measured how IPL batters actually respond, ball by ball, across every full-length chase in the archive from 2008 to 2026. The answer is clear and quite large. And it says something about an older idea too: whether some players are better chasers than others.

Measuring "behind the rate"

Runs needed and balls left are not enough on their own. Needing 60 off 36 balls is a very different job with two wickets down than with seven. So we measure pressure as the runs still needed, minus what an average side would expect to score from that point with those wickets in hand. A chase that is behind the rate needs more than that; one that is ahead needs less.

Each ball is compared with par for its over and wickets, and with the batter's own normal level for the season. That matters. Weak sides fall behind in chases more often, and weak batters score slowly anyway, so without comparing each batter with himself a weak side falling behind would look like a side choosing to bat slowly.

In the middle overs, a side 40 or more runs behind the rate scores +25 runs per 100 balls above its batters' normal level, and its batters get out 1.39 times as often, +39%. A side comfortably ahead does the opposite: 20 to 40 runs ahead, it scores −15 runs per 100 below normal and gets out −13% as often.

At the death the holding back is stronger still. A side 20 to 40 ahead going into the final overs scores −25 runs per 100 balls below normal, and its batters are out −26% less often. It has no reason to take risks, and does not.

In the powerplay the response is gentler. Even 40 runs behind, a side scores only +15 per 100 above normal, at 1.14 times the risk. Early in a chase there is still time, and the fielding restrictions already reward attacking.

One dial, not two

What stands out is that runs and risk move together. Batters do not find extra runs for free when they need them, and they do not become harder to dismiss without giving up runs. Behind the rate they buy runs with wickets; ahead of it they buy safety with runs. It is the same trade a new batter makes when he plays himself in, covered in what a batter's first ball costs, seen from the other side.

Is anyone a clutch chaser?

If some players were genuinely better chasers, it would show up as a pattern that repeats. A batter who did better in chases than in first innings one season would tend to do so the next season too. So we measured each batter's chasing lean (how he scored chasing, compared with how he scored batting first) and asked whether it carried over from one season to the next.

It does not. Across 354 pairs of consecutive seasons, the correlation between a batter's chasing lean one year and the next is −0.06, which is zero for any practical purpose. A player who looked like a great chaser one year was no more likely than anyone else to look like one the next.

That is not because nothing about batting repeats. Measured the same way, a batter's lean at the death carries over with a correlation of 0.44, and his lean against spin with 0.29. Death hitting and playing spin are real skills that show up year after year. Chasing, as a skill separate from batting well, is not.

So the reputation some players have as chasers is mostly a story told about good innings that happened to come in second innings. What does repeat is the way every batter responds to the scoreboard, shown in the table above.

In the game

The Fine Leg XI match engine reads the scoreboard in a chase the way real batters do. A side falling behind attacks and loses more wickets; a side well ahead knocks the ball around. That is why a big target in the game can collapse a chase, and why a modest one is often seen off with overs to spare. Try it in a drafted season.
