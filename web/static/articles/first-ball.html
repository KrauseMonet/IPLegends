What a batter's first ball costs

By Fine Leg XI · 23 November 2026

Every commentator says it: the hardest time to bat is the first few balls. A new batter is reading the pitch and the bowler, and the bowler is attacking him before he is set. It is one of the oldest ideas in cricket, but it is rarely measured, because measuring it properly is harder than it looks.

We measured it on every ball faced in a full-length IPL innings from 2008 to 2026, 281,389 balls in all. This is what it says about how long getting in takes, and what it costs.

Why the simple version is wrong

The obvious way to do this is to take every batter's strike rate on his first ball, his second, his tenth, and so on, and compare. That gives the wrong answer. A batter who reaches his thirtieth ball is more likely to be a good batter having a good day, so late balls look fast partly because only the better innings get that far. And the situation changes too: a batter on his first ball in the nineteenth over faces a different game from one on his first ball in the first over.

So we compare each batter with himself. Every ball is first compared with par, what an average batter would score from that ball given the over and the wickets down. Then each batter's own average for the season is taken off, so a strong player does not make long innings look fast. What is left is how this batter did on this ball compared with his own normal. Batters need 150 balls in a season to be their own yardstick.

The first ball

In the middle overs, a batter's first ball goes for −47 runs per 100 balls against his own average. Once he has faced fifteen balls he is scoring +4 per 100 above it. The gap is close to half a run a ball, on the ball when he is newest.

The same holds in every phase. In the powerplay the first ball is −29 runs per 100 balls against his own average; at the death it is −46, while a batter who is already set at the death scores +14 per 100 above his average. That last figure is why sides want a set batter at the crease for the final overs.

The recovery is quick but not instant. In the middle overs a batter is back to his own average by about ball 10. In the powerplay, with the field up and the ball hard, it takes about 5 balls, and at the death about 7.

What getting in costs a side

Add up the shortfall over a batter's first fifteen balls against his settled rate, and you get what a new batter costs his side in runs. Arriving in the powerplay it is about 0.74 runs. In the middle overs it is 1.91. At the death it is 2.64.

That is the hidden price of a wicket. A wicket does not just remove a batter; it replaces a set one with a new one, and the newcomer gives some runs back while he gets in. The cost is more than three times larger at the death than in the powerplay, which is one reason a late collapse hurts more than its wicket count suggests.

The surprise: new batters are harder to get out

If the start of an innings is the hardest time to bat, you would expect new batters to get out more. In the powerplay and middle overs they get out less. A batter on his first ball in the middle overs is dismissed 2.6% of the time; once he has faced fifteen balls it is 4.6% a ball. In the powerplay the figures are 2.7% and 4.5%.

These are plain dismissal rates, and if anything they understate the effect: the batters still in after fifteen balls are, on average, the better ones having the better days, and they are still out more often per ball.

The explanation is a choice, not a skill. A new batter defends: he takes fewer risks, and scores fewer runs because of it. A set batter attacks, scores faster, and gets out more often doing it. The same trade of runs for risk shows up in the chase, in how batters chase. At the death the pattern disappears: a new batter there is out 7.8% of the time against a set batter's 7.5%, because in the final overs nobody can afford to play himself in.

In the game

The match engine in Fine Leg XI plays this curve. A batter in your side starts slowly and safely, then speeds up and takes more risks once he is set, as real batters do. Players who settle unusually slowly, or unusually fast, carry that on their cards as well. You can see it for yourself by drafting a side in the draft and watching an innings ball by ball.
