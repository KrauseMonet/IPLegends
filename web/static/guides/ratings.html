How the ratings work

What a rating is

Every card in Fine Leg XI is one player's one season: Virat Kohli in 2016 is a different card from Virat Kohli in 2023. Each card carries a single number from 70 to 99. That number is not a guess, a poll or an official figure. It is worked out from the ball-by-ball record of every IPL match from 2008 to 2026, using the same data the season simulator plays with, so the number on the card and the way the player performs in your matches come from one source.

This page explains, in plain terms, how that number is reached: what is measured, how luck and small samples are kept in check, and why a card can read differently from a player's reputation.

Step one: what was the ball worth?

A single run means different things in different situations. A single off the last ball of the powerplay with no wickets down is ordinary; a single with nine down in the nineteenth over can be the difference between winning and losing. So the rating does not start from runs or wickets. It starts from a state model: for every over of an innings and every number of wickets down, the archive tells us how often a ball goes for a dot, a single, a two, a four or a six, and how often it takes a wicket. That gives an expected value for a ball bowled in that exact situation.

The model is fitted on full-length first innings only. Rain-shortened matches, super overs and the handful of overs where the umpire miscounted the balls are left out, because each would distort what an ordinary over looks like.

Every ball a batter faces is then compared with what an average batter would have scored in the same situation. Score more than par and you gain; get out and you lose what a wicket costs at that point of the innings. A wicket in the first over costs far more than one in the last, because it takes away all the runs a set batter would have gone on to make. A bowler's figures are the same comparison seen from the other end.

Two details matter here. A wide is not a ball faced, but a no-ball is, which is the scorers' own convention. And a batter is only charged for his own dismissal: being run out at the non-striker's end is not held against the striker.

Step two: separating skill from luck

Twenty overs is a short game, and a season is a small sample. A batter who faces 60 balls and scores 140 off them may be brilliant or may have had a lucky fortnight. The rating deals with this by shrinkage: each player-season is pulled towards the typical level of players in the same batting role, and the fewer balls a player faced, the harder the pull. A batter with 600 balls in a season is mostly judged on his own numbers; one with 30 balls is mostly judged on what players like him usually do.

The amount of pull was chosen by testing, not by taste. Too little and a handful of hot innings shoots a fringe player to the top of the list. Too much and every season of a great player blurs into his average, which would throw away exactly the contrast between a career-best year and an ordinary one that makes the cards interesting.

Nobody is left out for having played too little. Every player who batted or bowled for a squad gets a card; a thin season simply leans more on the prior.

Step three: comparing eras fairly

Scoring in the IPL has risen sharply. The average first-innings total was around 150 in the early seasons and is close to 190 in the most recent ones. Comparing raw numbers across those years would make every modern batter look great and every modern bowler look poor. So each season is centred on itself: a player is measured against the other players of his own season. A strike rate of 140 in 2009 and one of 170 in 2025 can both be excellent, because each is compared with the league it was achieved in.

The spread of each season is deliberately not rescaled. Some seasons look more spread out than others, but almost all of that difference turned out to be sampling noise, and rescaling by it would have reshuffled hundreds of cards for no reason.

Step four: from a ball to a match

A drafter picks a player for a match, not for a single delivery. An opener who is excellent per ball and faces forty balls a match contributes more than a finisher who is equally good per ball but faces ten. So the per-ball figure is multiplied by how many balls the player usually faced or bowled in a match. An all-rounder's batting and bowling are both measured in runs above or below par, so they simply add up.

The card then blends that measurement with the headline numbers fans remember, compared with the player's peers of the same season and role: runs per match, strike rate and average for a batter; wickets per match, season wickets and economy for a bowler. Seven parts in ten of the card come from those numbers and three from the ball-by-ball measurement, so a season that topped the run or wicket charts is never buried by a technicality. A small amount is added for Player of the Match awards, which are the one human judgement in the record. A part-time bowler's few overs only count on his card if they helped him.

Last comes a reputation floor: a player with a strong career cannot fall all the way to the bottom of the scale for one quiet or injury-hit season. It can lift a poor season part of the way towards his career level, but it never pulls a good season down, and it is capped so it cannot turn a lucky one-match season into a great card.

The 70 to 99 scale

The final figure is placed on a scale from 70 to 99. The scale is a straight line, so a gap of four points means the same everywhere on it. The bottom is set near the second percentile of all player-seasons and the top near the ninety-ninth, which leaves only about thirty cards in nineteen seasons at 99. There is no 100, because no season was perfect.

The 99s include seasons most fans would name: Chris Gayle in 2011, 2012 and 2013, Virat Kohli in 2016, Jos Buttler in 2022, Jasprit Bumrah in 2020 and 2024. A 99 bowler and a 99 batter are the same distance above average, measured in runs per match.

What the simulator actually plays

The card is the number you see; it is not the number the match engine uses. Inside a match each player is described by two numbers for each discipline: how quickly he scores (or concedes), and how often he gets out (or takes wickets). Every ball is drawn from the state model for that over and that score, then adjusted by the batter's and the bowler's two numbers. That is why a dashing batter who scores fast but gets out often plays that way in your season, and a careful accumulator plays the other way, even when the two cards carry the same rating.

The simulator is checked against the real archive: playing average sides against each other, it reproduces the real average first-innings total, the number of extras, the wickets that fall, and how many fours and sixes are hit.

Why a card can surprise you

  • It is one season, not a career. A great player's quiet year is a weaker card than his best year.
  • Situation matters. Runs made when a match was already decided are worth less than runs that changed it.
  • Volume matters. A player who batted at seven and faced a handful of balls cannot earn as much per match as an opener, however clean his hitting.
  • It is compared within the season. 500 runs in a high-scoring year is a smaller achievement than 500 runs in a low-scoring one.
